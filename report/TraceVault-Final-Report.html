1. Analysis of the Problem Statement

1.1 Problem Overview

People who are harassed, stalked or cheated online usually do have the evidence. It is just scattered, messy and easy to dispute, so their cases stall.

Take a college student being stalked by an ex-partner. Over three weeks she collects WhatsApp threats from two SIM cards, Instagram messages from fake accounts, an anonymous email, a voice note, and a bank statement showing ₹35,000 she paid under threat. That is 11 files, 5 formats and 3 languages (English, Hinglish, Kannada).

When she goes to the police or a lawyer, she has a phone gallery, not a case. Someone has to read every file, put events in order, work out that four accounts are one person, and match the chat demands to the bank debits. Nobody can prove the screenshots weren't edited.

Who it affects:

  • Victims and their families, who have to retell a painful story again and again.
  • Lawyers and NGO counsellors, who spend hours sorting files before any legal work starts.
  • Police cyber cells, who receive unstructured uploads and can't easily check whether files were changed.

1.2 Causes and Impact

Why the problem exists

  • Scattered: evidence sits in chat apps, screenshots, call recordings, emails and PDFs. No tool reads them together.
  • Multilingual: threats arrive in Hindi, Hinglish, Kannada and other languages that standard tools don't read.
  • Fragile: screenshots can be edited and timestamps disputed. Without a record of who handled the file, evidence gets challenged or rejected.
  • Unlinked: one offender uses many numbers, handles and UPI IDs. Connecting them by hand is slow and error-prone.
  • A new legal requirement: Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) expects a certificate with a hash value for electronic records. Victims have no easy way to produce one.

How serious it is

2.2 Identified Gap

Police tools have powerful analysis but are locked inside agencies. Victim apps are easy to use but only store files. Nothing gives a victim both.

No current tool does all of these together:

  1. Reads the evidence a victim already has (screenshots, chat exports, voice notes, PDFs), including Indian languages.
  2. Links the same offender across many accounts, and links chat demands to bank debits.
  3. Finds contradictions and edited files before the other side does.
  4. Backs every AI statement with a citation to the source file.
  5. Produces output that a court or police officer can check for tampering independently, even offline.

TraceVault fills this gap: police-grade analysis and proof, in the victim's hands.

3. Ideation of the Solution

3.1 Proposed Solution

Upload everything once. Get back a sealed case file where every claim points to its source.

4. Implementation Roadmap

4.1 Work Completed in 24 Hours

We built a working end-to-end prototype (web app, API server and AI service) and a test case to measure it against.

To test honestly, we generated a synthetic case with a known answer key: Ananya Rao v. Karthik Menon, a stalking, defamation and extortion case over 2–28 September 2024. It has 11 files and 3 deliberately planted problems: an edited screenshot, a chat amount that doesn't match the bank debit, and a wrong payment date in the victim's own statement. All names and numbers are fictional.

How far the prototype works

On the test case, the full pipeline (OCR, speech, language, extraction, case rebuild, agents) passed 36 of 36 end-to-end checks. Where it falls short:

  • The timeline has some noise, such as "Active 2m ago" from an Instagram profile tagged as a place, and the 9 Sep "please stop" message isn't its own event. Timeline order hasn't been scored against the answer key yet.
  • Some database integration tests were written but couldn't run in our build environment, and the collaborator flow and officer approve/reject buttons haven't been tested end to end yet.
  • Offence mappings in the complaint helper and regional guide stay hidden from users until a lawyer reviews them.

Challenges we faced

  • Reading Indian scripts. Our on-device OCR models only read English (and Chinese), so Kannada and Hindi screenshots came back empty. We added Google Vision and Sarvam Vision as cloud options, used only when the user's privacy setting allows it.
  • Too many false alarms. Early contradiction checks fired whenever two copies of the same chat disagreed slightly. We wrote strict rules: normalise amounts, dates and IDs first, and flag only when independent sources disagree about the same fact.
  • Translation changing meaning. Machine translation could shift a threat's meaning. We always show and cite the original, run number and ID extraction on the original text, and use the English copy only for analysis.
  • Dropping Docker and Redis. To make setup fast on every laptop, we replaced Redis with a job queue inside MongoDB, with retries, de-duplication and crash recovery.
  • Serverless deployment. Vercel functions can't run long background jobs, so jobs are claimed in time-boxed batches by a scheduled task. The AI service has to be hosted separately.
  • Free translation limits. Google's keyless translate endpoint started rate-limiting us, so we moved to the official API and Sarvam.
